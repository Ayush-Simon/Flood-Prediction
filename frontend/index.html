How to read this page

NYC Flood Watch forecasts how deep the water on New York streets will get in the next 5 to 40 minutes, from rain gauges and 27 street flood sensors.

Past storms replays a real 2026 storm. You see what the model forecast and what really happened, so you can judge it.

Live shows today’s weather and the forecast for the next 40 minutes. Switch with the Live and Past storms buttons at the top.

  1. Pick a stormUse the storm list at the top. The summary below tells you how much rain fell and how deep the water got.
  2. Stand inside the stormIn “Raining now”, move the time. Each sensor shows the water then, the forecast for 5, 15, 30 and 40 minutes later, and what really happened in grey.
  3. Watch the mapPress ▶ under the flood map to replay the storm. Red streets are the most likely to flood. Type a start and destination for a route around them.

Reading the numbers: depths are inches of water at the sensor. “dry” is under 0.05 in. Orange is some water, red is 4 in or more, which the site calls flooding.

    What the map colours mean

    Street colours are the street model’s risk of a flooding report in this storm: high risk means a 1% or higher chance, about 25 times the average street. The model learned from 311 reports, which over-rank areas where people report more, so it is a risk ranking, not measured water. When each street lights up and clears is a display rule that follows rain at the nearest gauge, not a depth forecast. Only the diamonds carry the depth model: its forecast at each FloodNet sensor. Routes ignore one-way rules.

    How it works, and how to read the results

    Every score on this page comes from storms the models never saw while learning (depth: July to October 2026). Here is how to use the page, what each model does, and what the numbers mean.

    Check the weather

    “Weather now” shows the rain falling and the forecast for the next 24 hours. Under 5 mm of rain forecast means no flooding is expected.

    Read the map colours

    Red, orange and yellow streets are high, medium and low flood risk from the street model. Diamonds are flood sensors with the depth model’s forecast.

    Move through time

    Press play or drag the slider under the map. Streets fill as rain builds and clear as it drains. Type a start and destination to get a route around high-risk streets.

    Check the sensors

    The side panel says how deep the water should be at each sensor 5, 20 or 40 minutes from now. In “Past storms” it also shows what really happened.

    The models

    ANN-LSTM

    First guess the rain, then guess the water.

    Last hour of rainat the nearest gauge
    →
    ANNforecasts the next 40 min of rain
    →
    LSTMreads past depth + rain + forecast
    →
    Depth5 to 40 min ahead

    The ANN is a small neural network: layers of simple maths units that learn how rain usually continues. The LSTM is a network with memory. It reads the readings in order, keeps what matters (water has been rising for 20 minutes) and forgets what does not.

    CNN-LSTM

    Spot short patterns first, then follow them over time.

    Last hourdepth and rain, every 5 min
    →
    CNN filtersslide over 15-min windows
    →
    LSTMfollows the patterns
    →
    Depth5 to 40 min ahead

    Each filter looks at three readings at a time and lights up for one shape, like a sudden burst of rain or water starting to climb. The LSTM then works from those shapes instead of the raw numbers.

    XGBoost

    Hundreds of small yes/no questions, added up.

    Featuresrain, drains, flood maps, past reports
    →
    Tree 1“more than 10 mm?”
    +
    Tree 2fixes tree 1’s mistakes
    + …
    Scoreflood chance or depth

    Each tree is a short chain of yes/no questions. Every new tree is trained on the errors of the ones before it, so the sum keeps getting better. XGBoost ranks which streets flood on the map, and is also used to forecast rain and depth.

    Persistence is the baseline: it assumes the water stays exactly as it is now. Any model worth using has to beat it.

    Stage 1: forecasting the rain (ARIMA, XGBoost, ANN)

    Before the water depth, the project forecasts the rain itself, hour by hour, 1 to 6 hours ahead, for each of the 8 rain grid cells over New York. Four models were compared on July–October 2026, hours the models never saw.

    Rain forecast accuracy (hours with rain)

    Typical miss (RMSE, mm of rain in the hour; lower is better) and R² (higher is better), at 1, 2 and 3 hours ahead. Only hours with rain in the last 3 hours or the forecast window, the ones that matter for floods. Persistence assumes this hour’s rain simply continues. XGBoost is best by a small margin; ARIMA and the ANN are close behind, and all beat persistence. Script 03.

    How ARIMA calculates the forecast

    ARIMA here is its autoregressive form, AR(p): the next hour’s rain is the long-run average plus a weighted sum of how far each of the last p hours was from that average.

    rainnext = μ + φ1(r1 − μ) + φ2(r2 − μ) + … + φ6(r6 − μ)

    1. Fit: for each rain cell, on the last two training years (2024–2025), try p = 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 and keep the one with the lowest AIC (a score that rewards fit and punishes extra terms). All 8 cells chose p = 6.
    2. Weights: φ1 ≈ 0.5 for the last hour, then small weights (under 0.12) for the hours before; μ ≈ 0.13 mm per hour. So rain mostly carries on at half its strength, drifting back to the average.
    3. Look further ahead: the 1-hour forecast is fed back in as if it had been measured, then the formula runs again for hour 2, and so on to hour 6. Negative results are set to 0.

    What the scores mean: R², RMSE, warnings

    What R² means

    R² compares a model with the laziest possible guess: the average depth. It asks how much of the gap between that guess and the real water level the model closes.

    Measured depthForecastAverage (R² = 0)
    0.50

    Worse than averagePerfect

    R² = 1 − (forecast’s squared errors ÷ the average’s squared errors).

    1.0perfect forecast
    0.9very close (5 minutes ahead)
    0.5gets the shape, misses the size (20 minutes)
    0 or lessno better than the average (40 minutes)

    RMSE (inches)

    The typical size of a miss. An RMSE of 0.65 in means forecasts are usually within about two-thirds of an inch. Lower is better.

    How accurate the models are

    These scores were measured once, on storms from July to October 2026 that the models never saw, by comparing their forecasts with what the sensors really recorded. They describe how far to trust the models, so they do not change with today’s weather. Today’s numbers are in Today’s prediction.

    What the results say

    • Water depth 5 minutes ahead is predicted well (R² 0.94). By 20 minutes the models get the shape of a flood but not its size.
    • The standard LSTM has the lowest error of the neural models, and the gap to ANN-LSTM and CNN-LSTM is statistically significant (p ≤ 0.01).
    • A simple physical “bucket” model with three numbers is just as accurate as the LSTM (no significant difference, p = 0.56).
    • Counted per flood, the rule “warn when water shows” catches 65% of floods about 10 minutes ahead, with half its alarms false.
    • The 40-minute forecast is not held back by the rain forecast: even the rain that really fell lifts its R² only from 0.11 to 0.19. The models judge how high a flood will get (R² 0.55 for the highest depth in the next 40 minutes) far better than the minute it peaks.
